Worn paper, faded markings, and irregular scales can give a pale background character. The useful question is how that surface behaves beside the content. A texture that looks subtle on its own may become distracting behind fine lettering or a detailed illustration.

## Choose the scale first

Start with a plain background and one texture. Try it at two noticeably different sizes while keeping the opacity unchanged. Small marks can read as grain; larger marks reveal the pattern. Choose the size that supports the composition before adjusting its strength.

For repeating textures, look across the whole page. A seam or an obvious repeated patch can draw attention even when the colour is faint.

## Reduce contrast gradually

### Compare with real text

Bring the texture closer to the background colour or lower its opacity a little at a time. Place real headings, body text, and images over it as you work. Judge the surface with that content present, especially behind the smallest text.

## Let some areas stay clear

The texture does not need equal strength everywhere. A softer area behind a paragraph can help it remain readable, while more visible marks near an edge give the surface definition. Use a gradual transition so the clear area feels connected to its surroundings.

## Review the full composition

- Read the smallest text at normal viewing size.
- Check the top and bottom of a repeating surface for visible seams.
- Compare the texture beside both sparse and detailed images.

> A background earns its place when the composition is easier to recognise, not harder to read.

Check both a narrow phone layout and a wider view. Compare the page with the texture switched on and off. Keep the version where the surface adds interest and the words remain easy to read. One well-judged layer may be enough.
